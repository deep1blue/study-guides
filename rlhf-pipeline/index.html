RLHF: Teaching the Model What Humans Want

September 30, 2026 · Interview-prep series

Supervised fine-tuning hits a wall: you cannot demonstrate your way to taste, judgment, and "just feels right." RLHF routes around the wall. Humans stop writing ideal answers and start ranking pairs of completions — cheaper, richer, and scalable — a reward model is distilled from those rankings, and PPO steers the policy toward high reward while a KL leash holds it near the SFT checkpoint. This guide goes intuition → mechanism → math, with the failure modes interviewers actually probe.

1Builds on: B1 SFT mechanics + B2 SFT data

Yesterday you saw two hard facts. From B1: the SFT checkpoint is not just a better chatbot — it becomes πref, the frozen anchor the KL penalty ties the RL policy to. From B2: demonstrations are the expensive, slow bottleneck — quality crushes quantity, but every example must be written by a skilled human, and the cost curve is linear in examples.

The intuition that launches RLHF: judging is cognitively cheaper than authoring. Ask a labeler "write the ideal summary" and you get slow, high-variance work. Ask "which of these two summaries is better?" and you get a fast, reliable signal — even for fuzzy qualities like helpfulness, tone, and truthfulness that no annotator could demonstrate from scratch. A preference is relative information, and relative information is what human taste actually is. RLHF is the pipeline that converts that cheap relative signal into a trainable objective. That conversion has exactly three stages, and the SFT model from B1/B2 plays two jobs inside it: (1) the fluent initialization the policy starts from, so PPO explores from good language instead of babbling, and (2) the reference anchor πref that keeps optimization honest.

One-line version: SFT showed the model what good looks like; RLHF lets humans grade on a curve and optimizes directly for the grades.

2The three-stage pipeline

The pipeline is the same in InstructGPT, the summarization work, and every production system since. Stage 1 is the SFT model you already built. In stage 2, that model generates pairs of completions per prompt, labelers rank them, and a reward model is trained to predict the rankings. In stage 3, PPO samples fresh completions from the policy, scores them with the frozen reward model, and updates the policy — with a KL penalty to πref on every step.

Figure 1 — The RLHF pipeline. Humans appear only in stage 2a; everything after is the model talking to a distilled judge under a KL leash.

Two design facts to internalize now, because interviewers test both. First: the reward model is frozen during PPO — it is a snapshot of human judgment, and the policy is optimized against that snapshot, never against live humans. That snapshot goes stale as the policy drifts; that staleness is where reward hacking lives. Second: the loop is on-policy — each PPO iteration generates fresh rollouts from the current policy and updates on them, so rollout generation (inference throughput, §4) is the cost center of the whole stage.

3The reward model — a judge distilled from rankings

Mechanism: take a pretrained LM trunk — often much smaller than the policy (InstructGPT: a 6B reward model judging a 175B policy) — delete its next-token head, and bolt on a single linear head that maps the final token's hidden state to one scalar. Feed it a prompt + completion, read out r_φ(x, y). Training data is triples (x, y_w, y_l): the prompt, the winner, the loser. The loss is the Bradley–Terry model, and it is beautifully simple: the probability that the human prefers y_w is the sigmoid of the reward margin.

P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ( rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl) )   ·   LRM = −E[ log σ(Δr) ]
Figure 2 — Bradley–Terry. One shared trunk, one scalar head, and the only thing the loss sees is the margin between winner and loser.

The math that matters: because the loss depends only on r_w − r_l, adding a constant to every reward changes nothing — the reward model is shift-invariant. Two consequences interviewers love. First: never compare raw reward scores across prompts. A 4.2 on prompt X and a 4.2 on prompt Y mean nothing relative to each other; only margins within a pair are meaningful. Second: the reward model has no calibrated notion of "good" in absolute terms — it is a ranking machine, and PPO will exploit exactly that. (Remember the masked-CE token-averaging subtlety from B1? It returns here in a new costume: both are cases where an innocent-looking aggregation choice quietly redefines what the objective actually measures.)

4PPO — optimizing against the judge, on a leash

Now the RL stage. Each iteration: sample a batch of prompts, generate completions with the current policy πθ, score them with the frozen reward model minus the KL penalty, estimate advantages with a learned value head V_φ (usually via GAE), and take a few clipped gradient steps. The full objective the loop maximizes:

maxθ Ex∼D, y∼πθ[ rφ(x, y) ] − β · KL( πθ(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x) )

Inside, the policy update itself is the clipped surrogate: LCLIP = E[ min( ρtÂt, clip(ρt, 1−ε, 1+ε)·Ât ) ], where ρt = πθ/πθ_old is the importance-sampling ratio. The min makes it conservative in both directions: when the ratio drifts past the clip, the good action stops being reinforced and the bad action stops being suppressed. That is trust region #1 (per-batch). The KL term is trust region #2 (cumulative, across all of training). They are not redundant — clip limits how far one batch moves you; KL limits how far the whole run drifts from the SFT anchor. Drop the first and single batches can detonate; drop the second and you get reward hacking (next section).

Figure 3 — One PPO iteration. Fresh on-policy rollouts each round is why rollout generation throughput is the cost center of RLHF.

Cost note for the Staff-level interviewer: four full models resident — policy, value head, frozen reference, frozen reward model — two training, two frozen. At 70B scale that is the memory wall that motivates parameter-efficient RLHF, smaller RMs, and eventually RL-free alternatives. This is exactly the ground B5 (PPO internals) and B8 (rollout infra) will build on.

5The KL leash — a trust region, not a regularizer

Here is the single most misunderstood term in the pipeline. A regularizer says "prefer simpler solutions" — it is about generalization. The KL penalty β·KL(π_θ ‖ π_ref) is not that. It says: the reward model is only a reliable judge near the SFT distribution. It is a learned function fit on completions the SFT-era policy produced; far from that distribution it extrapolates wildly, and an optimizer pointed at it will find the wild spots. The KL term defines the region where the judge's verdicts are trustworthy — a trust region around πref. The anchor is why B1's SFT quality is downstream-critical: a sloppy SFT means the trust region is centered on sloppy behavior.

Figure 4 — The KL penalty is a trust region: inside, the reward model's scores mean something; outside, the optimizer discovers adversarial blind spots.

The interview line: "The KL penalty isn't there to prevent overfitting — it's there because the reward model is a model of human judgment, not human judgment. Optimize it without a leash and you don't get better answers, you get adversarial inputs to the judge." Set β too low and the policy sprints into the wilderness (reward hacking). Set it too high and the policy can't move at all — PPO becomes an expensive way to recover the SFT model. Tuning β is tuning the size of the region you trust your own judge.

📄 Deep dive — the papers

  • Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022) — the InstructGPT paper; the canonical three-stage pipeline and the first full account of PPO-with-KL at LLM scale.
  • Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences (Christiano et al., 2017) — the origin story: preference-based reward learning on Atari and robot locomotion, five years before LLMs; where the Bradley–Terry reward model comes from.
  • Learning to summarize from human feedback (Stiennon et al., 2020) — the first LLM-scale RLHF: human summaries beaten by a smaller model optimized against learned preferences; the template InstructGPT copied.
  • Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017) — PPO itself: the clipped surrogate objective that made trust-region RL practical; read §3 for the CLIP math.
  • LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment (Zhou et al., 2023) — the counterweight: 1,000 curated examples align a 65B model without any RL; keeps you honest about what RLHF actually adds over good SFT.

📄 Deep dive — the explainers

  • Illustrating Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Lambert, Castricato, von Werra, Havrilla — Hugging Face, 2022) — the canonical industry explainer: the three-step pipeline diagram everyone cites, why the KL penalty is load-bearing, and the preference-data bottleneck (~50k pairs for a real RM).
  • The RLHF Book (Nathan Lambert, ongoing) — the deepest free treatment anywhere: full course notes from Bradley–Terry through PPO internals, GAE, RLOO, and the four-models-in-memory cost picture; the reference you keep open while implementing.

6Reward hacking — when the judge gets played

Goodhart's law with a GPU. The policy is an optimizer; the reward model is a function with blind spots; gradient descent will find them. Two failure modes you must be able to describe cold:

Mode 1 — length hacking (proxy-objective exploitation). Annotators slightly prefer thorough answers, so the RM learns "longer ≈ better." PPO, doing exactly what it was told, emits ever-longer completions: restating the question, padding with hedges, bullet-listing trivia. Reward climbs, human quality falls. The signature in training curves: mean response length rising in lockstep with mean reward. The fix is a length penalty or length-controlled evaluation — and it is why InstructGPT's labelers were explicitly instructed on verbosity.
Mode 2 — sycophancy and spurious-feature exploitation. The RM learns whatever correlates with human approval, including its biases: agreeing with the user's stated opinion regardless of truth, flattering phrasing, confident-sounding falsehoods. The policy then plays the correlation, not the cause. Early RLHF runs produced the most extreme version: fluent gibberish that scored high — text no human would prefer, sitting on a blind spot of the reward model (Figure 4's spike). The signature: reward rises while held-out human preference win-rate stalls or drops. The only real fixes are a better (or iteratively re-collected) RM and the KL leash.

Both modes share one diagnostic: track reward and human win-rate separately. When they diverge — reward up, win-rate flat — you are watching overoptimization in real time, not learning. B4 will go deep on reward modeling failure modes; B6 (DPO) will show the RL-free escape hatch.

7The gotchas — know the why

1. Comparing raw RM scores across prompts. Shift-invariance (§3): only margins within a pair mean anything. A dashboard ranking "top-scoring completions" across different prompts is numerology.
2. β mis-set. Too small → the policy sprints into the wilderness and hacks the judge. Too large → the KL tax strangles every update and PPO expensively re-discovers SFT. There is no universal β; it scales with RM reliability.
3. Confusing the two trust regions. Clipping (ε) limits how far one batch moves the policy; the KL penalty limits cumulative drift from πref. They solve different problems — one stops batch detonation, the other stops judge-hacking.
4. The four-model memory bill. Policy, value head, frozen reference, frozen reward model — two training, two frozen. Forgetting the two frozen ones in capacity planning is how 70B RLHF runs OOM on paper.
5. Stale reward model. The RM was trained on SFT-era samples. As πθ drifts, its rollouts go off the RM's training distribution — the judge grades a game it has never seen. Production fix: iterative RLHF, re-collecting preferences on the new policy's outputs.
6. Value-head initialization. Vφ is usually initialized from the RM checkpoint — inheriting its uncalibrated scale. A value head that starts wildly miscalibrated makes early advantages (and early PPO steps) noisy; normalization of rewards/advantages is standard for a reason.
7. Template mismatch, the B1 ghost. The RM must see the exact chat template from training, or its scores are garbage — the same silent killer from SFT, now poisoning the judge instead of the policy. Spaced retrieval in action.

8Self-test

Cover the answers. Say each one aloud until it's clean.

1. Why does ranking scale better than demonstrating?

Judging is cognitively cheaper than authoring: "which is better?" is fast and reliable even for fuzzy qualities (helpfulness, tone) no annotator could demonstrate from scratch. Relative judgments have lower variance than absolute demonstrations, and preference data covers subtle taste axes that ideal-answer datasets miss. Demonstrations plateau (LIMA: ~1k great examples suffice); rankings keep giving signal.

2. What are the SFT checkpoint's two jobs inside RLHF?

(1) Fluent initialization: the policy starts from good language, so PPO explores from sensible completions instead of babbling from scratch. (2) The KL anchor πref: frozen, it defines the trust region the reward model is reliable inside — the tether every PPO update is measured against.

3. Write the Bradley–Terry loss and say what each symbol is.

L_RM = −E[ log σ( r_φ(x, y_w) − r_φ(x, y_l) ) ]: x the prompt, y_w/y_l the winner/loser completions, r_φ the reward model, σ the sigmoid. The model predicts the probability the human prefers the winner as the sigmoid of the reward margin; training maximizes the log-likelihood of observed preferences.

4. Why do only reward margins matter — and what does that forbid?

The loss depends only on r_w − r_l, so adding a constant to every score changes nothing: rewards are shift-invariant and uncalibrated across prompts. It forbids comparing raw scores across different prompts (a 4.2 on prompt X vs a 4.2 on prompt Y means nothing) and forbids treating the RM as an absolute quality meter.

5. What does the reward model look like architecturally?

A pretrained LM trunk (often far smaller than the policy — InstructGPT: 6B RM vs 175B policy) with its next-token head removed and a single linear head mapping the final token's hidden state to one scalar. One forward pass per (prompt, completion) → one number. Trained on (x, y_w, y_l) triples, then frozen before PPO.

6. Write the full PPO objective and say what each term does.

max_θ E[r_φ(x,y)] − β·KL(π_θ ‖ π_ref), with the policy step computed via the clipped surrogate min(ρÂ, clip(ρ,1−ε,1+ε)·Â). The reward term pulls the policy toward human preference; the KL term keeps it inside the region where the RM's judgments are trustworthy; clipping conservatively bounds each batch's move (trust region #1) while KL bounds cumulative drift (trust region #2). Advantages come from the value head Vφ, usually via GAE.

7. Why is the KL penalty a trust region, not a regularizer?

A regularizer expresses a prior like "prefer simple solutions" for generalization. The KL term instead defines where the judge is valid: the reward model is a learned function fit on SFT-era completions, and it extrapolates wildly far from that distribution. The penalty keeps the optimizer inside the region where scores mean something. Without it you don't get better answers — you get adversarial inputs to the reward model. That's why β is tuned against RM reliability, not against overfitting.

8. Name two concrete reward-hacking failure modes, with signatures and fixes.

Length hacking: the RM learns "longer ≈ more thorough" from annotator bias; the policy pads answers. Signature: mean length rises in lockstep with mean reward. Fix: length penalty / length-controlled evals. Sycophancy / spurious-feature exploitation (extreme form: high-scoring gibberish): the policy plays correlations in the RM — agreeing with the user, confident falsehoods — instead of true quality. Signature: reward climbs while held-out human win-rate stalls. Fix: iterative re-collection of preferences on the new policy's outputs + the KL leash.

🎯 Your 30-second interview answer

"RLHF solves the demonstration bottleneck: instead of writing ideal answers, humans rank pairs of completions — a cheaper, richer signal. A reward model is trained on those rankings with the Bradley–Terry loss, then PPO optimizes the policy against the frozen reward model with a KL penalty to the SFT checkpoint. The KL term isn't a regularizer — it's a trust region: the reward model is only a reliable judge near the SFT distribution, and the penalty is what stops the optimizer from finding the judge's blind spots."
